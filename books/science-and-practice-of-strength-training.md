# Science and Practice of Strength Training

**Author:** Vladimir Zatsiorsky and William Kraemer
**Published:** 1995, later editions with Andrew Fry
**One line:** The bridge between Soviet era sports science and a working coach's programming
decisions, built around the idea that you can only train what you actually stimulate.

## Why I picked it up

Because "add five pounds" stops being an answer eventually, and I wanted the vocabulary for
what comes after.

## The core argument

Zatsiorsky's framing question is how to make a muscle produce maximal force, and he gives
three methods that are still the cleanest taxonomy I know:

- **Maximal effort.** Lift near maximal loads. Trains the nervous system to recruit high
  threshold motor units. Most specific to strength, most fatiguing, lowest volume.
- **Repeated effort.** Lift submaximal loads to or near failure. The last few reps recruit
  the same high threshold units because the earlier ones are fatigued. More volume, more
  hypertrophy, more time.
- **Dynamic effort.** Lift submaximal loads as fast as possible. Trains rate of force
  development rather than peak force. Low fatigue, high skill component.

None of the three is correct in isolation. The programming question is what proportion of
each, for whom, when.

Layered on top are the concepts that do the real work:

**Specificity and transfer.** Training effects transfer to other tasks in proportion to how
similar the movements are in force, velocity, and coordination. This cuts against the
assumption that general strength automatically becomes sport performance, and it is why
"functional" needs a definition before it means anything.

**The accommodation problem.** The response to any given stimulus decreases as the body
adapts to it. This is the theoretical reason periodization exists at all: not because
variety is fun, but because an unchanged stimulus stops being a stimulus.

**Timing of adaptation.** Fatigue and fitness after a session decay at different rates.
Fitness gains persist longer than fatigue does, which is what makes tapering work and what
makes the timing of load a real variable rather than an accounting detail.

**Force velocity relationship.** Force and velocity trade off against each other. Where you
train on that curve determines what you get, which is why maximal strength work and speed
work are not interchangeable.

## Ideas worth keeping

- The three methods as a checklist. Any program can be audited by asking which of the three
  it actually contains and in what ratio.
- **The stimulus has to exceed what you are already adapted to**, and that threshold rises.
  Progressive overload is a consequence of accommodation, not an independent rule.
- **Volume, intensity, and frequency cannot all rise at once.** Every program is a choice
  about which one to prioritize this block.
- Specificity is measurable, not rhetorical. Ask about force, velocity, and coordination
  overlap rather than whether an exercise looks like the sport.
- The distinction between what a muscle can produce and what the nervous system will let it
  produce. Much of early strength gain is the second, not the first.

## Where I disagree, or where it is thin

- **It is a textbook, and reads like one.** The practical chapters assume a coach with
  athletes, testing equipment, and a calendar, which most readers do not have.
- **Some of the underlying research is old** and comes from a system with selection effects
  and reporting practices that would not pass now. The frameworks have held up better than
  the specific numbers.
- **Periodization is presented with more certainty than the current evidence supports.** The
  literature comparing periodization models to well run non periodized progression is much
  less decisive than the book implies.
- Very little on the fact that most people's limiter is adherence and recovery, not model
  selection.

## What I will actually do

1. Audit my current program against the three methods and note which one is missing. It is
   usually dynamic effort.
2. Stop changing exercises out of boredom and start changing them when progress on the
   current stimulus flattens, with the log as the trigger.
3. Pick one measurable transfer target and test it every eight weeks, rather than assuming
   gym numbers are carrying over.

## Verdict

Not a program, a way of thinking about programs. Worth owning as a reference and reading in
sections rather than straight through. If Starting Strength answers what to do first, this
answers how to reason once the easy answers stop working.
